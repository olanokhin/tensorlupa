**In one sentence:** on children's stories it has never seen, micro-qwen3 guesses the next token right 52% of the
time, while a Markov chain built from the same stories guesses 42%.

## The question

A [language model](../../glossary/language-model/) does one thing: it scores every [token](../../glossary/token/) it
knows as the possible next piece of text. A simple way to ask how good it is: take a story the model has never
seen, stop at every position, and check whether the model's favourite token is the one the author actually wrote.
This share is called **top-1 accuracy**.

A number alone says little. 52% could be excellent or poor. So the model gets a rival that needs no neural network
at all: a table of counts.

## The rival: a Markov chain

The table is built from the same [TinyStories](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories) the model
was trained on. For every three tokens in a row, it remembers which token most often came next. To guess, it looks
at the last three tokens of the story and answers with the most frequent continuation. When those three tokens never
occurred in training, it falls back to the last two, and then to the last one.

This is a Markov chain: the next step depends only on a few steps right before it. It knows nothing about who the
story is about or what happened a paragraph ago.

## The result

Both were measured on the same text: about 404,000 tokens of held-out TinyStories V2 stories, which neither of them
saw during training.

| Who guesses | Looks at | Top-1 accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Markov chain (4-gram table) | the last 3 tokens | 42.1% |
| **micro-qwen3, 8-bit weights** | up to 255 previous tokens | **51.7%** |

The model wins by about 10 points: a quarter more right guesses. The difference is not in the easy places: after
"Once upon a" both say "time". It is in the places where three tokens are not enough.

## Where the extra 10 points come from

**Who the story is about.** After *"...there was a little girl named Lily. She loved to play outside. One day,"*
the table only sees "One day," and answers by habit:

| Next token | Markov chain | micro-qwen3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| she | 12% | **72%** |
| Lily | | 18% |
| he | **16%** | |
| they | 9% | |

The table's favourite is "he". The model remembers that the story is about Lily.

**What happened before.** After *"...a little boy named Tom. He had a red ball. One day, Tom lost his"*:

| Next token | Markov chain | micro-qwen3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| balance | **51%** | |
| toy | 18% | 13% |
| ball | 16% | **58%** |

In children's stories "lost his" is most often followed by "balance", so the table says so. The model has read the
red ball three sentences earlier.

When three tokens are enough, both agree. After *"Once upon a time, there was a little"*, the table says "girl"
(41%) and "boy" (30%); the model says "girl" (65%) and "boy" (25%).

## Why it can never be 100%

After "there was a little", "girl", "boy", "dog" and "bunny" are all correct. The author picked one, and any
predictor, a person included, misses the others. Top-1 accuracy counts a perfectly good alternative as a miss, so
it always stays well below 100%, for small and large models alike.

A measure that accounts for this is **perplexity**: the number of equally likely options the model is, on average,
choosing between. On held-out TinyStories the base micro-qwen3 has a perplexity of about 6.5. At every step it is
weighing roughly six or seven equally likely tokens out of 4,096. The same idea in bits is
[entropy](../../glossary/entropy/).

## The table still works in the studio

The same counts, kept for the token phrases that occur at least 50 times (about 2.3 MB), drive the studio's
[repetition penalty](../../glossary/repetition-penalty/): when the model is about to repeat a phrase that is rare in
real stories, the table flags it. The Markov chain does not write the stories, the model does. The chain only says
which repeats are natural.

## A bit of history

On 23 January 1913, Andrey Markov presented a study at the Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg. He had
taken 20,000 letters of Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*, the whole first chapter and sixteen stanzas of the second, and
counted by hand how vowels and consonants follow one another. It was the first application of what we now call a
Markov chain: the next symbol depends on the one before it.

In 1948 [Claude Shannon](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6773024) used the same idea to imitate English,
letter by letter and word by word. In 2003 [Yoshua Bengio and colleagues](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf)
replaced the counting with a neural network, and modern language models, micro-qwen3 among them, descend from that
step. This page puts the two ends of that line side by side, on the same stories.

## How it was measured

- **Text:** the validation part of TinyStories V2, about 404,000 tokens, not used to train either predictor.
- **Markov chain:** counts of 4-, 3- and 2-token sequences from the training stories, with the thresholds 5, 3 and 1
  occurrences; the longest context that is in the table wins. It has a guess at every position.
- **Model:** the published 8-bit weights, the same as in the studio, reading the text in windows of 256 tokens.
  It sees only the tokens before each position, as when it writes.
- **Score:** the share of positions where the top candidate equals the token that is actually there.
