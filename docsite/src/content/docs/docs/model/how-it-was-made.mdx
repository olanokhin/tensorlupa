title: How micro-qwen3 was made
description: The trail of decisions behind micro-qwen3, from the vocabulary to the decoding rules, with every branch we tried and why the winner won.
---

**In one sentence:** every size and setting of micro-qwen3 came from a fork where several options were tried and
one went further.

The weights are on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/olanokhin/micro-qwen3); the training code, the firmware
and the C core are in [micro-qwen3-esp32](https://github.com/olanokhin/micro-qwen3-esp32).

This page is the trail of those forks. The bright line is what the model has today. The faint branches were tried
and dropped, each with the number that decided it.

Most numbers are **bits per byte (bpb)** on held-out text: how many bits the model needs, on average, to predict
each byte of text it has not seen. Lower is better. Each variant was trained once, so differences under about
0.01 count as ties. Numbers from different datasets cannot be compared with each other.

## Vocabulary

How many [tokens](../../glossary/token/) should the model know? Grid at about 0.6M parameters on web text
([FineWeb-Edu](https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb-edu)).

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">2,048 tokens</span><span class="tr-val">1.747</span><span class="tr-note">pieces too small</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">4,096 tokens</span><span class="tr-val">1.710</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">8,192 tokens</span><span class="tr-val">1.785</span><span class="tr-note">the embeddings eat the budget of the layers</span></li>
</ul>

The vocabulary is a byte-level BPE trained on our own text with Qwen3's splitting rule. Qwen3's own 151,936
tokens would not fit: their table alone would be 16 times bigger than the whole model.

<li><span class="tr-name">15 layers × 48</span><span class="tr-val">1.744</span></li>

A faithful 28-layer miniature of Qwen3 was the worst. Qwen's number of layers does not scale down a thousand

## Outliers in the residual stream

A few channels of the [residual stream](../../glossary/residual-stream/) grow much louder than the rest: we called
them stalactites. The hypothesis came from audio. Before every block the stream is normalised by its overall
loudness, so a few loud channels push the quiet useful signal down, like one loud instrument in a mix. Two ways
to test it, on the 3 × 112 shape with 146M tokens of web text each:

- **A. Hold the peaks**, as in an audio chain: replace the normalisation before each block with a limiter or a
  compressor.
- **B. Keep the peaks from growing**: rotate the optimizer's updates, or change the optimizer.

The numbers after each branch: quality in bpb, then how loud the loudest channel is against a typical one.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">RMSNorm and AdamW, as in Qwen3</span><span class="tr-val">1.610</span><span class="tr-note">loudest channel 5.4×</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: limiter, tanh instead of the norm (Dynamic Tanh)</span><span class="tr-val">2.107</span><span class="tr-note">far worse</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: compressor, μ-law curve (asinh) before the norm</span><span class="tr-val">1.608</span><span class="tr-note">5.5×, peaks not held</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: compressor with ratio and a soft knee</span><span class="tr-val">1.608</span><span class="tr-note">8.8×, peaks even louder</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: Hadamard rotation of AdamW's updates</span><span class="tr-val">1.607</span><span class="tr-note">3.0×: tamer, the same quality</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: the same rotation in blocks of 16 or 4</span><span class="tr-val">1.609 to 1.611</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: Muon optimizer</span><span class="tr-val">1.909</span><span class="tr-note">2.5×, the quietest, but far behind with our settings</span></li>
</ul>

The outliers could be tamed, but quality did not move: at this size the loud channels were not stealing much from
the quiet ones. The model kept the plain Qwen3 recipe.

## Data

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">web text, 1.06 billion tokens</span><span class="tr-val">1.567</span><span class="tr-note">scored on web text; too hard for a million parameters</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">TinyStories, first version</span><span class="tr-val">0.769</span><span class="tr-note">scored on the same V2 stories as the next row</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">TinyStories V2</span><span class="tr-val">0.669</span><span class="tr-note">stories written by GPT-4</span></li>
</ul>

A billion tokens is a thousand per parameter, fifty times the usual rule of about twenty, and the model still
could not hold the web: it learned grammar but not what to say. Children's stories have a small world that a million
parameters can describe.

The two TinyStories rows are scored on the same held-out V2 stories. V2 is narrower, not simply better: the model
trained on it is a specialist. It is stronger on V2 stories (0.669 against 0.769) and weaker on stories of the first
version (0.933 against 0.765).

Where the data comes from: the web text is [FineWeb-Edu](https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb-edu), and both versions of the stories are in the
[TinyStories](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories) dataset by Ronen Eldan and Yuanzhi Li: the first has stories written by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, V2
only by GPT-4.

## How many bits per weight

The finished 3 × 112 model on web text (1.599 bpb), each weight rounded to fewer bits with one scale per row, no
further training. Loss against full precision:

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">8 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.000</span><span class="tr-note">lossless: the studio version</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">6 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.003</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">5 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.014</span></li>
<li class="tr-open"><span class="tr-name">4 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.066</span><span class="tr-note">half the size, the first real loss: the board version, see the next two forks</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">3 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.431</span><span class="tr-note">the text falls apart</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">2 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+3.17</span></li>
</ul>

Plain rounding is free down to 6 bits and breaks below 4.

## Fewer bits, bigger model?

Is a bigger model with fewer bits per weight better, at the same memory on the board (about 0.8 MB)? The ternary
branch followed the Bonsai recipe: weights of −1, 0 or +1 with a learned scale per row.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">ternary layers, trained from scratch, 3 × 192</span><span class="tr-val">0.704</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">ternary layers, from full precision + QAT</span><span class="tr-val">0.697</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">2-bit layers, 3 × 192</span><span class="tr-val">0.698</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">3-bit</span><span class="tr-note">the same quality per byte as 4-bit, a more complex kernel</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">4-bit, 3 × 144</span><span class="tr-val">0.643</span></li>
</ul>

Below 4 bits quality drops in a step, and more training data does not break that floor. 4 bits is the best that
fits the budget.

## Making 4 bits work

How to round the weights to 4 bits and lose as little as possible. Loss against full precision, on the base
model, 100 MB of further training for the trained variants.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">plain rounding</span><span class="tr-val">+0.045</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a scale per 32 weights, as in GGUF Q4</span><span class="tr-note">slower than 8 bits on the chip</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">per-row scale search, no training</span><span class="tr-val">+0.037</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">standard quantization-aware training (QAT)</span><span class="tr-val">+0.029</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">Hadamard rotation baked into the weights</span><span class="tr-note">needs a separate output layer, +458K parameters</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">Hadamard rotation after QAT</span><span class="tr-note">worse on the final model</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">QAT with the per-row scale search (W4R)</span><span class="tr-val">+0.022</span></li>
</ul>

For each row of weights, the search tries 25 clipping levels and keeps the one with the smallest error. Inside
QAT, the model learns weights that survive that rounding.

## Training

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">pretraining from scratch on TinyStories V2</span><span class="tr-val">124M tokens</span><span class="tr-note">next-token prediction</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">instruction tuning with QAT from the first step</span><span class="tr-val">150M tokens</span><span class="tr-note">900,000 dialogues</span></li>
</ul>

These are two passes of one training, one after the other, not two options. The second pass teaches the model to
write a story with the requested words, in the Qwen chat format. Of the requested words, 5.7% made it into the
story before it and 64% after it. On held-out answers the model needs 0.747 bits per byte with 8-bit weights
and 0.762 with 4-bit weights, which is 2% more. The dialogues come from
[TinyStoriesInstruct](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStoriesInstruct) by the same authors, converted to the Qwen chat format.

## Spelling automaton

The bonus decides *when* a word appears, not *how* it is spelled. A small model can start a rare word and wander
off halfway, or glue on more letters: "examineineine".

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">bonus only</span><span class="tr-note">broken words</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">an FSM whose word forms glue every ending onto every word</span><span class="tr-note">allows "dareing", "invitationing": 6.0% of stories with an invented word</span></li>

## Cutting the tail

[Top-k](../../glossary/top-k/) keeps 40 candidates, and over a story the draw sometimes lands on one with a chance
of 0.003% and starts a word that does not exist ("Byican"). Stories with an invented word, 300 stories, with the
repetition penalty, 8-bit / 4-bit weights:

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">top-k 40 only</span><span class="tr-val">2.7% / 6.7%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">top-p 0.9</span><span class="tr-val">3.3% / 1.3%</span><span class="tr-note">the strongest cut, the least diverse text</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">top-p 0.95</span><span class="tr-val">1.3% / 3.3%</span><span class="tr-note">a tie with min-p 0.02</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">min-p 0.05 or 0.1</span><span class="tr-val">1.7–2.3% / 1.7%</span><span class="tr-note">less diverse</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">min-p 0.02: drop what is 50 times less likely than the favourite</span><span class="tr-val">1.0% / 3.0%</span></li>
</ul>

Min-p and top-p tied at the same strength; min-p won on clarity, since its line sits at a fixed distance from the
favourite. How it works: [Min-p](../../glossary/min-p/).

## Corpus statistics

A table of four-token phrases counted in the training stories, used next to the model. One file serves two
jobs, so its size was chosen for both: phrases seen at least 50 times, 2.3 MB.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">no repetition penalty</span><span class="tr-note">looping stories 17%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a simple penalty for any recent repeat (the board keeps it)</span><span class="tr-note">looping 4%, pushes hard words into invented ones</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a penalty graded by how common the phrase is</span><span class="tr-note">looping 1%</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">common phrases (1,000+ times in the corpus) are free, other repeats lose 4 points</span><span class="tr-note">looping 0%</span></li>
<li class="tr-open"><span class="tr-name">draft tokens for speculative decoding, from the same file</span><span class="tr-note">1.84 tokens per model check: the next fork</span></li>
</ul>

The penalty lets "once upon a time" repeat and stops "the tree was very tall" from coming back. How it works:
[Repetition penalty](../../glossary/repetition-penalty/). The draft guesses the next few tokens from the table, and
the model checks them all in one pass; a separate 14 MB draft table did only slightly better (1.87). On the board
this could one day mean speed; in the studio it would be a way to watch guessing and checking.

## Speed and repeatability on the chip

The same C core runs on the board and on a computer. Two questions: how fast a full story is on the ESP32-S3 (one
core, Wi-Fi on), and whether a seed found on a computer writes the same story on the board.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">exp and round from the C library, compiler free to fuse multiply and add</span><span class="tr-val">63 tok/s</span><span class="tr-note">the board and a computer agree on 4 of 7 stories</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">own exp (a 6-line polynomial) and rounding, each input quantized once</span><span class="tr-val">76 tok/s</span><span class="tr-note">same quality, still 3 of 7</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">the same, with fused multiply-add off on both sides</span><span class="tr-val">74 tok/s</span><span class="tr-note">30 of 30 stories identical, letter for letter</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">output layer shortlist over the top bit of the weights (lookup tables)</span><span class="tr-val">56 tok/s</span><span class="tr-note">every story kept, but slower</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">shortlist over the top two bits, vector kernel, codes in fast memory</span><span class="tr-val">72 tok/s</span><span class="tr-note">the KV cache has to leave fast memory</span></li>
</ul>

The stories differed because the chip and the computer computed the exponent with different library code, and one
side fused multiplications with additions. With an exponent built from plain + and ×, and fusing off on both sides,
a seed found on a laptop with the same C code (`host/order` in the board repository) writes the same story on the board. The studio uses its own decoding rules (a repetition penalty from corpus statistics), so its stories differ from the board's anyway.

The output layer is a third of the time of a token on the board, and it waits for memory: a row read from internal
SRAM is 2.1 times faster than one from PSRAM. A shortlist reads only the top bits of every row, picks 256
candidates and computes them exactly. It kept every one of 200 stories, but on this chip the first pass over all
4,096 rows, choosing the 256 best and the lack of fast memory for both the codes and the KV cache cost more than
it saved.
