title: How micro-qwen3 was made
description: The trail of decisions behind micro-qwen3, from the vocabulary to the decoding rules, with every branch we tried and why the winner won.
---

**In one sentence:** every size and setting of micro-qwen3 came from a fork where several options were tried and
one went further.

This page is the trail of those forks. The bright line is what the model has today. The faint branches were tried
and dropped, each with the number that decided it.

Most numbers are **bits per byte (bpb)** on held-out text: how many bits the model needs, on average, to predict
each byte of text it has not seen. Lower is better. Each variant was trained once, so differences under about
0.01 count as ties. Numbers from different datasets cannot be compared with each other.

## Outliers in the residual stream

A few channels of the [residual stream](../../glossary/residual-stream/) grow much louder than the rest: we called
them stalactites. The hypothesis came from audio. Before every block the stream is normalised by its overall
loudness, so a few loud channels push the quiet useful signal down, like one loud instrument in a mix. Two ways
to test it, on the 3 × 112 shape with 146M tokens of web text each:

- **A. Hold the peaks**, as in an audio chain: replace the normalisation before each block with a limiter or a
  compressor.
- **B. Keep the peaks from growing**: rotate the optimizer's updates, or change the optimizer.

The numbers after each branch: quality in bpb, then how loud the loudest channel is against a typical one.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">RMSNorm and AdamW, as in Qwen3</span><span class="tr-val">1.610</span><span class="tr-note">loudest channel 5.4×</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: limiter, tanh instead of the norm (Dynamic Tanh)</span><span class="tr-val">2.107</span><span class="tr-note">far worse</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: compressor, μ-law curve (asinh) before the norm</span><span class="tr-val">1.608</span><span class="tr-note">5.5×, peaks not held</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">A: compressor with ratio and a soft knee</span><span class="tr-val">1.608</span><span class="tr-note">8.8×, peaks even louder</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: Hadamard rotation of AdamW's updates</span><span class="tr-val">1.607</span><span class="tr-note">3.0×: tamer, the same quality</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: the same rotation in blocks of 16 or 4</span><span class="tr-val">1.609 to 1.611</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">B: Muon optimizer</span><span class="tr-val">1.909</span><span class="tr-note">2.5×, the quietest, but far behind with our settings</span></li>
</ul>

The outliers could be tamed, but quality did not move: at this size the loud channels were not stealing much from
the quiet ones. The model kept the plain Qwen3 recipe.

A billion tokens is a thousand per parameter, fifty times the usual rule of about twenty, and the model still

parameters can describe.

## How many bits per weight

The finished 3 × 112 model on web text (1.599 bpb), each weight rounded to fewer bits with one scale per row, no
further training. Loss against full precision:

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">8 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.000</span><span class="tr-note">lossless: the studio version</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">6 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.003</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">5 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.014</span></li>
<li class="tr-open"><span class="tr-name">4 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.066</span><span class="tr-note">half the size, the first real loss: the board version, see the next two forks</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">3 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+0.431</span><span class="tr-note">the text falls apart</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">2 bits</span><span class="tr-val">+3.17</span></li>
</ul>

Plain rounding is free down to 6 bits and breaks below 4.

## Fewer bits, bigger model?

Is a bigger model with fewer bits per weight better, at the same memory on the board (about 770 KB)? The ternary
branch followed the Bonsai recipe: weights of −1, 0 or +1 with a learned scale per row.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">ternary layers, trained from scratch, 3 × 192</span><span class="tr-val">0.704</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">ternary layers, from full precision + QAT</span><span class="tr-val">0.697</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">2-bit layers, 3 × 192</span><span class="tr-val">0.698</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">3-bit</span><span class="tr-note">the same quality per byte as 4-bit, a more complex kernel</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">4-bit, 3 × 144</span><span class="tr-val">0.643</span></li>
</ul>

Below 4 bits quality drops in a step, and more training data does not break that floor. 4 bits is the best that
fits the budget.

## Making 4 bits work

How to round the weights to 4 bits and lose as little as possible. Loss against full precision, on the base
model, 100 MB of further training for the trained variants.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">plain rounding</span><span class="tr-val">+0.045</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a scale per 32 weights, as in GGUF Q4</span><span class="tr-note">slower than 8 bits on the chip</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">per-row scale search, no training</span><span class="tr-val">+0.037</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">standard quantization-aware training (QAT)</span><span class="tr-val">+0.029</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">Hadamard rotation baked into the weights</span><span class="tr-note">needs a separate output layer, +458K parameters</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">Hadamard rotation after QAT</span><span class="tr-note">worse on the final model</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">QAT with the per-row scale search (W4R)</span><span class="tr-val">+0.022</span></li>
</ul>

For each row of weights, the search tries 25 clipping levels and keeps the one with the smallest error. Inside
QAT, the model learns weights that survive that rounding.

## Training

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">pretraining from scratch on TinyStories V2</span><span class="tr-val">124M tokens</span><span class="tr-note">next-token prediction</span></li>

</ul>

The second pass teaches the model to write a story with the requested words, in the Qwen chat format. All three
words made it into the story in 5.7% of stories before it and in 64% after it. The 4-bit version loses about
2% of quality against 8 bits.

## Logit bias

The model wants the requested words but rarely wants them enough to win. A bonus to their
[logits](../../glossary/logits/) closes the gap. Stories with all three words, out of 200 test orders:

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li><span class="tr-name">no bonus</span><span class="tr-val">36.5%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a constant bonus</span><span class="tr-val">79.5%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">a big constant bonus, +6</span><span class="tr-note">less natural text, words still missed in 8.5%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">each used word raises the bonus for the rest</span><span class="tr-val">94%</span></li>
<li><span class="tr-name">the bonus also grows while a word waits</span><span class="tr-val">99.5%</span></li>
<li class="tr-win"><span class="tr-name">the same rule at temperature 0.6</span><span class="tr-val">100%</span></li>
</ul>

The full story is in [Logit bias](../../glossary/logit-bias/).

## Spelling automaton

The bonus decides *when* a word appears, not *how* it is spelled. A small model can start a rare word and wander
off halfway, or glue on more letters: "examineineine".

## Markov chain: the next fork

A table of word sequences counted in the training stories, used next to the model. Measured, not in the model
yet.

<ul class="tr-branches">
<li class="tr-open"><span class="tr-name">repetition penalty that knows the corpus</span><span class="tr-note">looping stories 21% → 3%, no broken words</span></li>
<li class="tr-open"><span class="tr-name">draft tokens for speculative decoding</span><span class="tr-note">1.45 to 2.1 tokens per model check</span></li>
</ul>

The penalty punishes a repeated phrase only when it is rare in real stories, so "once upon a time" stays free.
The draft guesses the next few tokens from the table, and the model checks them all in one pass.
